Fermat, the greatest mathematician at his time, claims he has proven his last theorem $a^n + b^n = c^n$.

\section{Euclid's definition of a point}
\begin{quote}
  \textit{To anyone who has lived in a modern American city
  (except Boston) at least one of the underlying ideas of
  Descartes' analytic geometry will seem ridiculously evident.
  Yet, it took mathematicians all of two thousand
  years to arrive at this simple thing.}
  \hfill
  Bell, pp. 123
\end{quote}

Really, it seems obvious to us that to describe points
on a two-dimensional plane we may use \textit{two dimensions},
the horizontal and the vertical. This makes it rather surprising
that such a representation was not developed any sooner
than the fifteenth century, such as by the Ancient Greeks who
are especially known for their study of geometry.

For argument's sake, let us assume that the Greeks noticed
this idea, but for some reason which we will explore,
it was never popularized or documented at the time.
After all, the basic idea was no more than just
comparing the widths and heights of rectangles who share
a corner:

\begin{figure}[H]
  \begin{center}
    \input{fig1.tex}
  \end{center}
  \caption{What the Ancient Greeks might have drawn.}
  \label{fig:1}
\end{figure}

It is known that the Ancient Greeks were quite capable
in the study of the lengths in geometrical configurations,
with examples like Archimedes' approximation of $\pi$.
In fact, for the task of computing the distance between
points $A$ and $B$ in \Cref{fig:1}, we would use none
other than the \textit{Pythagorean} theorem, a testament
to the Greeks' geometrical development.

Yet they never achieved the level of abstraction attained
by the cartesian plane, which would turn out to be a very
powerful tool. It could reduce the proof of or disproof of any
claim to a purely mechanical routine that required no
spatial intuition, much unlike the classical methods that
relied on studying the diagram.

Notwithstanding the difference in available mathematical knowledge,
I argue that a reason for the delay in the invention of analytic
geometry is the way in which mathematical knowledge was conveyed
in the ancient times and at Descartes' time.
Specifically, Ancient Greeks studied mathematics through spoken
persuasion and with reference to geometrical diagrams and construction,
differing from the modern approach of symbolic abstraction, which was
already popularized at Descartes' time.

Because of the spoken tradition of presenting mathematical knowledge,
there is an inherent risk of overlooking certain important details.
Consider Euclid's definition of a point as an example.
In his list of definitions in Book 1, Euclid writes:

\begin{quote}
  \small
  \textgreek{Σημεῖόν ἐστιν, οὗ μέρος οὐθέν.}
  \hfill
  \textit{A point is that of which there is no part.}
\end{quote}

Such a definition might be acceptable at the time. Euclid's students
likely did not challenge this definition, because it states what is
intuitively the distinction between a point and all other geometrical
objects. You can always divide a curve or a solid into parts, but
there is no way to do this to a point.
Moreover, this definition was, at the time, rather inescapable: Another
characteristic of a point is that it has only position. Yet this
risks a circular argument, since positions are simply points.

The oral tradition is the ultimate cause for this weakness of
the Ancient Greek, since obviously for the sake of persuasion such an
intuitively obvious definition is preferred, over some convoluted way
of describing a point as side lengths of rectangles as drawn in
\Cref{fig:1}.
This is a significant reason why Euclid chose not to define a point
as `represented by the pair of numbers $(x, y)$'.

For this reason, Ancient Greek geometers would never uncover the full
power of their geometrical knowledge, which lies in Descartes' invention
of the \textit{coordinate system} that would unite geometry with algebra.
The Pythagorean theorem was a way to compute hypotenuses of right
triagles, but it would not become \textit{The Distance Formula} until
Descartes.
