\section{Problem Statements}
\begin{problem}[Baltic Way 2021] \label{prob:1}
  We are given 2021 points on a plane, no three of which are collinear. Among any 5 of
  these points, at least 4 lie on the same circle. Is it necessarily true that at least 2020 of the points
  lie on the same circle?
\end{problem}

\vspace{1em}\begin{problem}[IMO shortlist 1999] \label{prob:2}
  A circle is called a \textit{separator} for a set of five points in a plane
  if it passes through three of these points, it contains a fourth point in its
  interior, and the fifth point outside the circle.

  \noindent Prove that every set of five points such that no three are collinear and no
  four are concyclic has exactly four separators.
\end{problem}

\vspace{1em}\begin{problem}[Baltic Way 2019] \label{prob:3}
  There are 2019 points given in the plane. A child wants to draw $k$ (closed) discs in such a manner,
  that for any two distinct points there exists a disc that contains exactly one of these two points. What is the
  minimal $k$, such that for any initial configuration of points it is possible to draw $k$ discs with the above property?
\end{problem}

\vspace{1em}\begin{problem}[Baltic Way 2024] \label{prob:4}
  There is a set of $N\geq 3$ points in the plane, such that no three of them are collinear.
  Three points $A, B, C$ in the set are said to form a \textit{Baltic triangle} if no other point in the set lies on the circumcircle of triangle $ABC$.
  Assume that there exists at least one Baltic triangle.
  
  \noindent Show that there exist at least $\frac{N}{3}$ Baltic Triangles.
\end{problem}

\subsection{Motivation for Inversion}
\begin{figure}[H]
  \begin{center}
    \input{fig1.tex}
  \end{center}
  \caption{Reflecting a point over a line}
  \label{fig:1}
\end{figure}

Firstly, let us intuitively motivate the upcoming definition of an inversion.
We can easily reflect a point across a given line: draw a normal from point onto the line,
and mark the refection as the point on this normal which has an equal distance to the line.
In \Cref{fig:1} point $P'$ is the reflection point $P$ across line $l$.

Now, what if instead of a line we had a circle instead?
How should reflection be defined in that case?
This premise may seem odd, but it will turn out to be a surprisingly powerful tool if defined
appropriately.

Let us first try to define reflection over a circle in `the obvious way':
Like with the line, draw a normal from the point onto the circle, and mark the reflection as
the point on this normal which has an equal distance to the point where the normal intersects
the circle.
This is quite simple to do, since the normal to the circle from a point is simply the 
line connecting it with the centre.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \begin{center}
    \input{fig2.tex}
  \end{center}
  \caption{Naïvely reflecting over a circle}
  \label{fig:2}
\end{figure}

\vspace{-1em} In \Cref{fig:2}, point $O$ is the centre of circle $\omega$, and line $OP$ is the normal drawn from point $P$.
With this definition, point $P'$ is the reflection of $P$ across $\omega$.
Though this looks reasonable so far, upon attempting to make reflections for
more points, we realise the drawbacks of such a guileless definition.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \begin{center}
    \input{fig3.tex}
  \end{center}
  \caption{Exposing the weakness of the naïve definition}
  \label{fig:3}
\end{figure}

\vspace{-1em} Again, points $A', B'$ are the reflections of $A, B$, and likewise $A'', B''$ are the reflections of $A', B'$.
But making observations from \Cref{fig:3} we realise some undesireable implications of this definition.

It feels wrong that $A$ and $A'$ lie on the same side of the circle (the two sides of a circle are `inside' and `outside') even though $A'$ is the reflection of $A$.
Moreover, though points $A'$ and $B'$ are the reflections of $A$ and $B$, points $A$ and $B$ themselves are not the reflections of $A'$ and $B'$.
What even is the reflection of $O$?
It is not possible to define it uniquely since it could be point $B$, or that point rotated with any angle.
Worse still, if point $M$ moves along the segment $AB$, then its reflection traces some complicated curve with endpoints $A', B'$, instead of something familiar like a line or a circle.

This last issue in particular cripples this definition of reflecting over a circle.
When we reflect many points over a line, they still stay the same shape:
lines map to lines, circles map to circles; it is not possible for simple things to have complicated reflections.

We need to define the reflection over a circle in a more deliberate way to avoid these undesirable weaknesses.
Since we call the operation `reflection', it should share some of the most fundamental properties fulfilled when reflecting over a line.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Property \arabic*.}, leftmargin=*]
  \item \label{prop:existence-uniqueness}
        The reflection of each point exists and is uniquely defined.
  \item \label{prop:involution}
        Reflecting the reflection of a point yields the point itself.
  \item \label{prop:fixed-line}
        Points on the line $l$ are fixed, i.e. reflect to themselves.
  \item \label{prop:opposite-side}
        Points not on the line $l$ are reflected to the opposite side of $l$.
\end{enumerate}

We can take these properties directly as the requirements for our definition of reflecting over a circle,
just by replacing line $l$ with circle $\omega$.
We have commented on how the naïve definition fails to satisfy \textbf{Properties 1, 2,} and \textbf{4},
but \textbf{Property 3} is satisfied.

Our note about the reflection of segment $AB$ in \Cref{fig:3}, however, warrants another property to be demanded
from the upcoming definition:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Property \arabic*.}, leftmargin=*, start=5]
  \item \label{prop:image-locus}
        If a point moves along a circle or a line, then its reflection also traces a circle or a line.
\end{enumerate}

Turns out \textit{inversion} is a way to define reflection across a circle which satisfies these properties.
In fact, the words are synonymous by convention, and
the author apologises for his abuse of terminology in the previous section.

\subsection{Definition of Inversion}

The author finds no better way to formulate of the definition of inversion than simply taking an 
extended quotation from his favorite mathematics textbook, \textit{Euclidean Geometry in Mathematical Olympiads} (2016).

\begin{quote}
  \hspace{-1em}\vrule width 1pt\hspace{1em}%
  \begin{minipage}[t]{0.95\linewidth}
    \small
    \setlength{\parindent}{1em}
    \setlength{\baselineskip}{0.7\baselineskip}
    \noindent The idea is to view every line as a circle with infinite radius.
    We add a special point $P_\infty$ to the plane, which every ordinary line passes through (and no circle passes through).
    This is called the \textbf{point at infinity}.
    Therefore, every choice of three distinct points determine a unique circle or line---three ordinary points determine a circle, while two ordinary points plus the point at infinity determine a line.
  

    With this said, we can now define an inversion. Let $\omega$ be a circle with centre $O$ and radius $R$.
    We say an \textbf{inversion} about $\omega$ is a map (that is, a transformation) which does the following.
    \begin{figure}[H]
      \begin{center}
        \input{fig4.tex}
      \end{center}
      \caption{\small $A\text{*}$ is the image of point $A$ when we take an inversion about $\omega$}
      \label{fig:4}
    \end{figure}

    \begin{itemize}
      \item The centre $O$ of the circle is sent to $P_\infty$
      \item The point $P_\infty$ is sent to $O$.
      \item For any other point $A$, we send $A$ to the point $A\text{*}$ lying on ray $OA$ such that $OA\cdot OA\text{*} = r^2$.
    \end{itemize}
    \hfill {\color{gray} \footnotesize Copied from \cite{Chen2016}}
  \end{minipage}
\end{quote}
